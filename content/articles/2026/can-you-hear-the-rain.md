Last month I put up a guitar improvisation. Six minutes and forty-one seconds, acoustic, one take. The title is "Can you hear the rain" because you can. It is in there the whole time.

<iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/2X3_4mouAFw" title="Can you hear the rain" loading="lazy" allow="accelerometer; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

An improvisation is a piece of music without a map, yet cogent. Without a map is the easy half. Cogent is the part that takes twenty years. Twenty years of theory, ear training, finger work, and listening -- truly listening, which is harder than playing and gets practiced less. The video is what that looks like when it catches: twenty years of trying to capture a feeling, in one take.

If you write software, that should sound familiar. Nobody improvises by skipping the fundamentals. You improvise because you did them until they stopped being a checklist. The senior engineer with no design doc is not winging it. The design is in the hands.

It does not take an orchestra. One guitar is enough. The notes are not the point. You are trying to land a feeling, and the instrument is the only interface you have for it. Distill it far enough and the feeling is the only thing left in the room. A bad interface makes a bad landing.

Capturing it is the fragile part. A take does not die from noise, it dies from attention. The moment you start watching yourself play, the thing you were playing stops. There is no undo. Replaying the last bar to catch it back does not work, because the state is the thing, and you cannot command the state from outside it.

Getting to that distillation took more than one instrument and more than one style. Ear training, theory, a second instrument, and hours of somebody telling you what they heard that you did not. Critique is part of the practice. You need ears that are not yours.

The trap is learning too much music. Learn every standard and you can play any room, and you will sound like the room. The repertoire starts writing for you. All of it -- theory, styles, the second instrument -- has to stay in service of noise. When it stops serving the noise, it is just material.

What lands in the take is a moment: practiced, confident, unreproducible. Play it again and you get a different piece. That is the trade. I did not stop when the rain started. There was no dry version. There was only the one with the rain in it.
